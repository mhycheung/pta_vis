Why doesn't the integral average to zero if the wave is periodic?

There are two integrals here, and neither one has to vanish.

1. The delay of one pulse is an integral along its path, not over a whole GW period. A pulse emitted at the pulsar and received at time t has delay R(t) = ∫ s(t', x(t')) dt' along its path. As it travels, it moves through the wave pattern. Because both the pulse and the wave move at c, the pulse passes through N = L(1 + cosα)/λ wave cycles on the way, not L/λ. The integral of a cosine over N cycles vanishes only when N is a whole number. Otherwise the left-over fraction of a cycle gives a net delay (see the path panel). Press "Set integer cycles along path": the residual becomes zero at all times. If the GW travels in the same direction as the pulses (α = 180°), N = 0: the pulse rides one point of the wave, but there the line-of-sight strain is zero because GWs are transverse.

Doing the integral gives two terms: R(t) = F h₀/(k(1+cosα)) [sin(kt) − sin(k(t − L(1+cosα)))], with F = ½sin²α. The first term depends only on the wave at Earth (the Earth term), the second only on the wave at the pulsar when the pulse left (the pulsar term). For real pulsars L is thousands of light-years and λ is a few light-years, so the two terms have unrelated phases and do not cancel.

2. The residual is a time integral of the period change, and the integral of a periodic function is periodic, not zero. The pulse period is shifted by δP/P = [sEarth(t) − spulsar(t − L)]/(1 + cosα), which oscillates with zero mean. The timing residual is the running total of these period shifts, R(t) = ∫ δP/P dt. The integral of cos(kt) is sin(kt)/k: it returns to its starting value after each period, so the residual does not grow, but in between it is not zero. Its amplitude is about h/(2πf), i.e. h times the GW period divided by 2π. This is why PTAs measure arrival times (the integrated quantity): a strain of 10−15 at a period of a few years gives residuals of order 10 nanoseconds.

In a real timing analysis the fitted timing model also absorbs a constant offset, a linear drift and a quadratic in time, so only the oscillating part of R(t) is detected.
